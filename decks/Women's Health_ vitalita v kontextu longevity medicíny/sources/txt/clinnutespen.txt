The menopausal transition typically occurs between 45 and 55 years of age and may extend over several years before and shortly after the final menstrual period. It is characterized by increasing menstrual irregularity resulting from hormonal fluctuations and may be accompanied by vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes and night sweats), sleep disturbances, mood changes, fatigue, and changes in body composition. Although symptom presentation is highly heterogeneous, vasomotor symptoms are among the most commonly reported manifestations and may persist for several years beyond menopause [ 3 ]. From both a clinical and public health perspective, this period represents a major challenge. Systematic reviews and meta- analyses have shown that an earlier age at menopause is associ - ated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, cardiovas - cular mortality and all-cause mortality [ 4 ]. These findings highlight the need to consider the menopausal transition not merely as an endocrine event, but as a critical period for the pre - vention of long-term chronic diseases. In parallel, menopausal symptoms, particularly vasomotor symptoms, are still predominantly approached through their impact on quality of life. However, several syntheses of the liter - ature suggest associations between the presence of vasomotor symptoms and a less favourable cardiometabolic pr ofile, alter - ations in endothelial function, and an increased risk of mood dis - orders, although these associations remain heterogeneous and partly influenced by confounding factors [ 5 ]. Longitudinal data notably indicate a bidirectional relationship between vasomotor symptoms and depressive symptoms during the menopausal transition [ 6 ]. These observations invite a shift beyond a purely symptom-centred interpretation of menopause, towards consid - ering certain symptoms as potential clinical markers of underlying vulnerability, while acknowledging the heterogeneity and limita - tions of the available evidence. In this context, interest in nutritional interventions during menopause has increased substantially. Nevertheless, the nutri - tion literature remains fragmented, often structured around iso - lated nutrients, specific symptoms or individual risk factors, which may lead to reductionist or excessively prescriptive messages. This approach contrasts with clinical reality, in which women under - going the menopausal transition frequently present with inter - twined issues, including emerging cardiovascular risk factors, abdominal fat redistribution, declines in muscle mass and func - tion, concerns regarding bone health, as well as sleep and mood disturbances. The aim of this review is to examine the role of nutrition during the menopausal transition and its potential implications for car - diometabolic health, bone and muscle health, and menopausal symptoms, in order to provide a clinically oriented framework for prevention. 2. Methodological positioning and methods This review is conducted as a critical narrative review, with the aim of providing a clinically oriented synthesis of the available evidence on the role of nutrition during the menopausal transi - tion. This methodological approach was chosen in order to inte - grate data from heterogeneous fields, including endocrinology, cardiometabolic health, bone and muscle health, and clinical nutrition, which are difficult to combine within a single systematic review, while allowing for a contextualised interpretation that is directly translatable to clinical practice. 2.1. Literature search strategy A literature search was conducted using the PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase and Cochrane Library databases, covering the period from their inception through 2025 (inclusive). The last literature search was performed in December 2025. This choice aimed to include the major historical data that have contributed to the under - standing of health trajectories during the menopausal transition. Search terms combined keywords related to menopause and the menopausal transition (“menopause”, “menopausal transi - tion”, “perimenopause”) with nutrition-related terms (“diet”, “nutrition”, “dietary patterns”, “Mediterranean diet”, “DASH diet”) and outcome-related terms (“cardiovascular risk”, “metabolic health”, “bone health”, “muscle mass”, “sarcopenia”, “vasomotor symptoms”). These terms were adapted to each database as appropriate. The reference lists of selected articles and relevant reviews were also screened to identify additional publications. This review did not follow a PRISMA-based wor kflow and no formal study selection flow diagram was produced, as the objec - tive was to provide a clinically integrative narrative synthesis rather than an exhaustive systematic mapping of the literature, which would have required a different methodological design. 2.2. Selection criteria and data prioritisation Priority was given to • systematic reviews and meta-analyses, • large longitudinal cohort studies, • randomised controlled trials when available and relevant. Observational studies with a lower level of evidence, cross- sectional studies, and small-scale trials were included selectively when they provided relevant mechanistic, pathophysiological or clinical insights, particularly in areas where high-level evidence remains limited. When multiple publications addressed the same topic, prefer - ence was given to studies with greater methodological robustness, adequate follow-up duration, larger sample sizes, and clinically meaningful outcomes (cardiovascular events, metabolic parame - ters, bone and muscle density or function, quality of life). Study selection was guided by clinical relevance, methodolog - ical robustness, and consistency of findings across publications, rather than predefined quantitative eligibility thresholds. The literature search, study selection, and qualitative analysis were conducted by the author, with the aim of clinically inte - grating the available evidence rather than pursuing exhaustive coverage. When evidence specifically addressing women during the menopausal transition was limited, relevant data from broader populations (including postmenopausal women or mixed midlife cohorts) were considered and interpreted cautiously within a clinically oriented framework. 2.3. Critical appraisal and clinical interpretation As this work is a narrative review, a formal grading of the level of evidence or strength of recommendations was not performed. No formal risk-of-bias assessment tool was applied systematically, in line with the objectives of this critical narrative review. Menopausal transition: hormonal changes and health trajectories The menopausal transition corresponds to a period of pro - gressive endocrine remodeling, characterized by an early alter - ation of luteal function with a decline in progesterone secretion, related to an increased frequency of anovulatory cycles, followed by a fluctuating and subsequently sustained decrease in ovarian estradiol production. These hormonal changes are accompanied by a progressive rise in gonadotropins, particularly follicle- stimulating hormone (FSH), as well as a shift in the estrogen- androgen balance, with peripheral estrogen production becoming proportionally more prominent as ovarian function declines. These changes extend far beyond reproductive function and exert systemic effects on multiple target tissues, including adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, bone, the cardiovascular system, and the brain. Data from large longitudinal cohort studies suggest that, in some women, this period is associated with an acceleration of certain cardiometabolic risk trajectories, independently of chro - nological aging [ 7 ]. 3.1. Hormonal changes and metabolic desynchronization Contrary to a simplified view of menopause as an abrupt and uniform decline in estrogen levels, data from longitudinal studies indicate that the menopausal transition is characterized by marked interindividual variability, with transient phases of rela - tive hyper- and hypo-estrogenism [ 7 , 8 ]. Hormonal instability is accompanied by an early decline in progesterone secretion [ 3 ], related to the increasing frequency of anovulatory cycles, as well as by alterations in the estrogen – androgen balance [ 6 ], with pe - ripheral estrogen production becoming proportionally more prominent as ovarian function declines. From a metabolic perspective, this hormonal desynchroniza - tion is associated, in some women, with a preferential redistri - bution of fat mass toward the abdominal compartment, changes in lipid profile, and impaired insulin sensitivity [ 7 ]. The clinical expression of these changes remains highly variable and is strongly influenced by associated factors such as weight trajectory, body composition, physical activity level, pre-existing metabolic status, and lifestyle habits [ 9 ]. Data from large cohort studies suggest that these changes occur more frequently during the menopausal transition than during chronological aging alone, supporting the hypothesis of a modulatory role of hormonal status in certain cardiometabolic risk trajectories, without a systematic or uniform pattern [ 1 ]. 3.2. From hormonal changes to cardiometabolic risk trajectories Longitudinal data suggest that the menopausal transition rep - resents a period during which certain cardiometabolic risk tra - jectories may shift unfavorably, including blood pressure, lipid parameters, and glucose metabolism. These associations are not uniform, however, and occur within a broader context in which aging and lifestyle-related factors also play a major role [ 3 , 4 ]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of observational studies indicate that premature or early menopause is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality. In contrast, evidence regarding the independent role of time since menopause onset on cardiovascular outcomes remains limited and heteroge - neous, precluding definitiv e conclusions about its long-term impact on cardiovascular risk, despite the biologically pivotal na - ture of the menopausal transition [ 2 ]. Taken together, these findings support the notion that meno - pause should not be considered an isolated risk factor, but rather as a potential accelerator of pre-existing cardiometabolic risk trajectories, shaped by genetic, behavioral, nutritional, and envi - ronmental determinants. From this perspective, prevention cannot be limited to late-stage management of established risk factors, but should instead be embedded within an anticipatory and individualized approach, ideally initiated during the menopausal transition. 3.3. Bone and muscle health and functional vulnerability The menopausal transition is associated with an acceleration of bone loss, particularly pronounced during the first years following the decline in estrogen production. Longitudinal studies show that this phase corresponds to a period of rapid loss of bone mineral density, with medium-term consequences for fracture risk [ 4 , 10 ]. In parallel, declining estrogen levels may contribute to increased vulnerability of skeletal muscle tissue, without sys - tematically leading to overt impairments in muscle mass, strength, or physical performance around the menopausal transition [ 11 – 13 ]. . Available evidence suggests that the clinical expression of this vulnerability is largely influenced by factors such as age, physical activity level, protein intake, and metabolic context [ 14 , 15 ]. In this regard, the menopausal transition may represent a period of heightened sensitivity to the deleterious effects of sedentary behavior, insufficient nutritional intake, or comorbid conditions, with potential long-term implications for physical function and autonomy. These observations underscore the relevance of considering bone and muscle health as a functional continuum, and of priori - tizing the maintenance of function as a central clinical objective from the menopausal transition onward, rather than waiting for the emergence of late manifestations such as frailty or established sarcopenia. 3.4. Cardiometabolic health: nutrition as a modulator of risk trajectories The menopausal transition may be accompanied, in some women, by an unfavourable shift in the cardiometabolic profile, characterised in particular by a deterioration in lipid metabolism during the transition to postmenopause, while other parameters (adipose tissue distribution, glycaemic regulation, blood pressure) tend to evolve more progressively and through multifactorial mechanisms [ 8 ]. These changes are neither uniform nor inevitable but rather occur within risk trajectories shaped by genetic, behavioural, and environmental determinants. In this context, nutrition should not be considered as an isolated therapeutic intervention, but rather as a lever for risk modulation, integrated within a comprehensive preventive strategy. 4.1. Dietary patterns and cardiovascular risk during menopause Mediterranean-type dietary patterns are characterized by a high intake of fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, olive oil, and fish, together with a reduced consumption of ultra-processed foods and saturated fats [ 17 ], whereas the DASH model empha - sizes a plant-rich and low-sodium diet, primarily aimed at reducing blood pressure [ 18 ]. The most robust available evidence relates to overall dietary patterns rather than isolated nutrients. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses show that higher adherence to Mediterranean or DASH-type dietary patterns is associated with a significant reduction in cardiometabolic risk, including type 2 diabetes, as well as lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, particularly among middle-aged and postmenopausal women [ 19 , 20 ]. These benefits are mainly driven by improvements in lipid profile, re - ductions in blood pressure, and better glycaemic regulation, without being attributable to any single dietary component [ 19 ]. Among women undergoing the menopausal transition, cohort studies highlight a specific period of cardiometabolic vulnerability, notably characterised by a transient deterioration in lipid profile during the late menopausal transition [ 1 ]. 4.2. Nutrition, glucose metabolism and insulin resistance: a contextualised approach As discussed previously, impairment of insulin sensitivity is neither systematic nor exclusively attributable to menopause. Nevertheless, among women presenting with abdominal fat redistribution, overweight, or a family history of type 2 diabetes, the menopausal transition may represent a period of increased metabolic vulnerability [ 8 , 21 , 22 ]. Systematic reviews indicate that nutritional interventions focusing on improving the quality of carbohydrate intake — - through reductions in refined carbohydrates and increased dietary fibre from minimally processed or unprocessed foods — as well as on overall dietary balance, are associated with modest but statis - tically significant improvements in glycaemic parameters [ 23 , 24 ]. However, these effects appear to be strongly dependent on baseline metabolic status, physical activity level, and long-term adherence, highlighting the importance of an individualised and contextualised approach. 4.3. Blood lipids: expected effects and limitations of nutrition Changes in the lipid profile observed during the menopausal transition, particularly increases in LDL cholesterol, do not result from a single or uniform mechanism. Rather, they refle ct hetero - geneous metabolic trajectories that are at least partly independent of chronological ageing [ 1 ]. In this context, meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials indicate that nutritional interventions can contribute to modest but clinically meaningful improvements in lipid profil es, particu - larly through a reduction in saturated fatty acids in favor of cis- unsaturated fatty acids, which is associated with a decrease in LDL cholesterol and in the total cholesterol/HDL cholesterol ratio [ 25 ]. In addition, other meta-analyses indicate that a diet higher in dietary fiber and minimally processed plant-based foods, including in particular legumes and certain whole grains, is asso - ciated with a modest but statistically significant reduction in LDL cholesterol [ 26 ]. However, these effects remain variable and, on their own, insufficient to normalize a markedly altered lipid profile. This limitation should be clearly stated in order to avoid any over - interpretation of the role of nutrition, which here falls within a framework of primary prevention or risk reduction, rather than substitution for pharmacological treatments when these are indicated. 4.4. Body weight, body composition, and cardiometabolic risk Weight gain frequently attributed to menopause largely reflects age-related factors, lifestyle changes, and a decline in physical activity. Nevertheless, in some women, the menopausal transition is accompanied by a preferential redistribution of fat mass toward the abdominal compartment, independent of total body weight, thereby contributing to an increased cardiometabolic risk [ 8 ]. Available data suggest that a lifestyle-based approach primarily targeting weight stabilization and reduction of abdominal adiposity (notably assessed by waist circumference) may improve cardiometabolic risk factors, even in the absence of substantial weight loss [ 27 ]. This pragmatic strategy, centered on measurable objectives, appears particularly relevant during the menopausal transition, a period characterized by heterogeneous cardiometabolic trajec - tories [ 1 ]. 4.5. Role of nutrition within an integrated prevention strategy Overall, nutrition appears to act as a modulator of car - diometabolic trajectories, with its impact closely dependent on the timing of intervention, the clinical profile, and the life context of each woman. Its potential seems greatest when implemented early, ideally during the early stages of the menopausal transition before the establishment of adverse cardiometabolic trajectories, and when combined with regular physical activity, including both aerobic and muscle-strengthening activities, as well as the man - agement of other risk factors. Bone and muscle health: nutrition in support of functional maintenance The menopausal transition represents a critical period for bone health, marked by a transient acceleration of bone remodeling related to the decline in estrogen production. Longitudinal studies show that this phase concentrates most of the bone loss, occurring around the time of the final menstrual period, and contributes to an increased subsequent risk of osteoporosis and fractures [ 10 , 26 ]. However, individual bone trajectories remain heterogeneous and are strongly influenced by modifiable factors, among which nutrition and physical activity play a central role. 5.1. Bone health: nutritional intakes, prevention, and limitations Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that adequate calcium and vitamin D intakes are necessary for the maintenance of bone health in menopausal women, particularly in the presence of deficiency or insufficiency . However, available evidence shows that calcium and vitamin D supplementation alone is not sufficient to prevent fractures in all women, underscoring the need for an integrated approach [ 28 , 29 ]. Beyond these micronutrients, overall diet quality appears to be a key determinant. Mediterranean-type dietary patterns have been associated with higher bone mineral density and a reduced risk of fractures in several observational studies and narrative syntheses, although interventional data remain limited. These effects appear to be mediated by higher intakes of fruits and vegetables, high-quality protein sources, and unsaturated fatty acids, as well as by a reduction in overall inflammatory burden [ 30 ]. 5.2. Muscle, sarcopenia, and functional vulnerability Contrary to a common assumption, the menopausal transition does not systematically lead to a loss of muscle mass or muscle strength. However, available evidence suggests that the decline in oestrogen levels may contribute to increased vulnerability of skeletal muscle through effects on protein synthesis, muscle tissue composition, and adaptation to exercise. Emerging evidence also suggests that declines in muscle strength may occur earlier than measurable reductions in muscle mass during the menopausal transition, highlighting the importance of functional outcomes when assessing early muscle vulnerability. The clinical expression of this vulnerability largely depends on factors such as age, level of physical activity, protein intake, and the underlying metabolic context. Available expert consensus statements suggest that pro - tein intakes of approximately 1.0 – 1.2 g/kg/day, combined with regular resistance exercise, may help support muscle mass and function in ageing adults [ 14 ]. However, specific evidence focused exclusively on women during the menopausal transition remains limited. In addition, oestrogen decline may contribute to reduced anabolic sensitivity to dietary protein, reinforcing the relevance of combining adequate protein intake with resistance exercise during this period [ 31 ]. Adequate protein intakes, distributed throughout the day, are associated with the maintenance of muscle mass and function, particularly when combined with regular physical activity including resistance training. From a practical perspective, these findings support the importance of ensuring sufficient protein intake at each main meal and of combining nutritional strategies with regular resistance exercise in order to optimise muscle pro - tein synthesis during the menopausal transition. In menopausal women, such strategies appear more relevant than isolated nutritional interventions aimed at increasing muscle mass, as the primary objective is the preservation of function and physical performance. 5.3. From mass to function: a primary clinical objective Clinical relevance does not lie solely in the preservation of bone or muscle mass, but rather in the maintenance of function, including strength, balance, mobility, and the ability to perform activities of daily living. Longitudinal studies indicate that func - tional performance is a stronger predictor of morbidity and loss of autonomy than body mass alone [ 32 ]. In this context, nutrition should be considered as a support for the maintenance of function, in synergy with physical activity, rather than as an isolated corrective strategy. This approach allows nutritional objectives to be reframed around clinically meaningful outcomes, particularly during the menopausal transition, a period in which early interventions may help limit the development of longer-term frailty trajectories. 5.4. Limitations of nutritional interventions and integration with other strategies It is essential to emphasize that nutritional interventions, although necessary, are not sufficient on their own to prevent osteoporosis, sarcopenia, or functional decline. Their effectiveness depends on the clinical context, long-term adherence, and their integration with other strategies, particularly adapted physical activity and, when indicated, pharmacological treatments. Acknowledging these limitations contributes to a more realistic approach to care and helps avoid ov ersimplified or excessively prescriptive narratives. It also allows nutrition to be repositioned within a comprehensive strategy for prevention and functional maintenance, consistent with the available scientific evidence. 6. Vasomotor symptoms: role of nutrition and clinical interpretation Vasomotor symptoms are among the most frequent and visible manifestations of the menopausal transition. They are tradition - ally addressed primarily in terms of their impact on quality of life, particularly because of their effects on sleep, daytime fatigue, and psychological well-being. While this perspective is legitimate, it tends to isolate symptoms from their broader pathophysiological and clinical context [ 5 ]. 6.1. Vasomotor symptoms and health trajectories Several studies suggest that the presence and severity of vasomotor symptoms may be embedded within broader health trajectories, particularly cardiometabolic and psychological ones. Associations have been reported between vasomotor symptoms, endothelial dysfunction, less favorable cardiometabolic pr ofiles, and depressive symptoms. The relationship between vasomotor symptoms and mental health appears to be bidirectional, high - lighting the complexity of the underlying mechanisms [ 33 ]. However, it should be emphasized that these associations do not allow the establishment of a direct causal relationship. Vaso - motor symptoms should therefore be considered as potential clinical signals, possibly indicating underlying vulnerability, rather than as independent risk factors or isolated therapeutic targets. 6.2. Role of nutrition in the management of vasomotor symptoms Interest in nutritional interventions aimed at reducing vaso - motor symptoms has increased substantially, particularly with regard to specific foods, nutrients, or plant-derived supplements. From a clinical perspective, it is useful to distinguish between evidence related to overall dietary patterns, individual foods, and specific bioactive compounds, particularly phytoestrogens, which represent the main focus of most intervention studies in this area. However, evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicates that the effects of these nutritional approaches on the frequency and severity of vasomotor symptoms are overall modest, heterogeneous, and often of limited clinical relevance [ 34 , 35 ]. Nutritional approaches may contribute, in some women, to a partial improvement in symptom-related comfort when imple - mented within a high-quality overall dietary pattern and an in - tegrated lifestyle approach that includes physical activity and broader lifestyle measures. However, nutrition cannot be consid - ered either an alternative to hormone therapy when indicated or a universal solution for vasomotor symptoms [ 36 ]. 6.3. Symptoms, patients’ expectations, and the risk of overinterpretation The strong expectations of patients regarding non-hormonal solutions expose both patients and clinicians to a risk of over - interpreting the benefits of nutritional interventions and to the dissemination of ov ersimplified messages that may be insuffi - ciently supported by the available level of evidence. This situation may lead to restrictive strategies that are difficult to sustain over the long term, or to excessive self-blame when symptoms persist. In this context, the role of healthcare professionals is to re- position nutrition within a realistic framework, clearly dis - tinguishing what relates to global health support from what per - tains to symptomatic treatment. A nuanced approach makes it possible to address patients’ expectations while avoiding the promotion of unfounded promises. 6.4. Toward an integrated interpretation of symptoms Considering vasomotor symptoms from an integrated perspective makes it possible to move beyond an exclusively symptom-focused approach and to embed their management within a broader prevention strategy. This perspective encourages a comprehensive assessment of associated risk factors, including lifestyle, diet, sleep, stress, and mental health, and opens the way to personalized interventions tailored to each woman's clinical profile and individual priorities [ 37 ]. Nutrition is presented as a modifiable lifestyle component that may contribute to the modulation of cardiometabolic trajectories, bone health, muscle function and, although more modestly and inconsistently, vasomotor symptoms, within an integrated strat - egy that also includes physical activity, sleep and stress manage - ment, and medical treatment when indicated. 7. Defining priorities: toward a pragmatic framework for nutritional management during the menopausal transition The preceding sections highlight the complexity of interactions between hormonal changes, health trajectories, and lifestyle determinants during the menopausal transition. In this context, an effective nutritional approach cannot be uniform, nor can it focus on a single symptom or isolated risk factor. Instead, it requires a clear prioritization of objectives, grounded in the level of available evidence and in medium- and long-term clinical relevance. 7.1. Prioritizing nutritional objectives During the menopausal transition, nutritional priorities can be organized into three complementary levels. The first priority concerns the preservation of function, including muscle and bone function and, more broadly, overall functional capacity. Maintaining mobility, autonomy, and physical function represents a central objective, given its major impact on quality of life and on the long-term risk of frailty. From this perspective, nutrition primarily aims to ensure sufficient and balanced intakes, in coherence with physical activity, rather than to correct isolated biological markers [ 28 – 32 ]. The second priority relates to the modulation of car - diometabolic trajectories. In women presenting with early risk factors or increased vulnerability, dietary interventions represent a relevant lever to limit the progression of cardiovascular and metabolic risk. This objective fits within a framework of primary prevention or risk reduction and does not replace pharmacological treatments when these are indicated [ 19 , 26 ]. The third priority concerns symptom management, particularly vasomotor symptoms. Although nutrition may contribute, in some women, to partial improvement in symptom-related discomfort, it should not be presented as a universal solution or as a systematic alternative to validated treatments. This hierarchical approach helps avoid excessive focus on symptoms at the expense of longer- term health priorities [ 33 , 37 ]. This framework may help clinicians distinguish whether car - diometabolic risk modulation, functional preservation, or symptom-related support should be considered the primary objective of nutritional intervention during the menopausal transition. 7.2. Identifying clinical profiles for which nutrition is particularly relevant Building on this hierarchical framework of objectives, it ap - pears that the relevance and impact of nutritional interventions vary according to clinical profile and timing of implementation. Nutrition therefore seems particularly relevant in women pre - senting with abdominal fat redistribution, early cardiometabolic risk factors, reduced physical activity, or insufficient or unbalanced dietary intakes. In these situations, it represents an accessible lever to modify certain risk trajectories before the development of established pathologies [ 1 , 9 , 21 , 22 ]. Conversely, when nutritional intervention is implemented at a later stage, in the context of already established diseases or in the absence of adherence to lifestyle changes, its impact appears more limited and should not be expected to function as an isolated therapeutic lever. This differentiated approach allows nutrition to be embedded within a targeted and realistic strategy, to tailor messages to in - dividual clinical priorities, and to avoid generic recommendations with limited effectiveness. It thereby strengthens the clinical relevance and translatability of nutritional interventions during the menopausal transition. M.-C. Baraut Clinical Nutrition ESPEN 75 (2026) 103405 6

7.3. Clarifying the role of nutrition within an integrated prevention strategy From this perspective, nutrition cannot be considered as an isolated intervention. Its effectiveness is closely dependent on its integration into a comprehensive strategy that includes physical activity, sleep and stress management, and, when indicated, hormonal or pharmacological treatments. The menopausal transition represents, in this regard, a particularly favourable window of opportunity to initiate or reinforce these interventions in a coordinated manner, before the establishment of risk trajectories that are more difficult to modify [ 1 , 9 ]. Clarifying the true role of nutrition also helps to prevent the drift associated with ov ersimplified or guilt-inducing narratives, often centred on excessive promises. This clarification contributes to repositioning nutritional care as a tool for reasoned prevention, grounded in the available scientific evidence, integrated within a multimodal approach, and adapted to the clinical priorities and individual expectations of women [ 37 ]. What nutrition can and cannot do during the menopausal transition What nutrition can do – Contribute to the modulation of cardiometabolic risk trajec - tories, particularly when implemented early in women with emerging risk factors. – Support the maintenance of muscle and bone function, in synergy with physical activity, notably through adequate pro - tein intake and an overall high-quality diet. – Contribute, in some women, to a partial improvement in symptomatic comfort, particularly vasomotor symptoms, within the context of an integrated lifestyle approach. – Act as a lever for reasoned prevention, promoting sustainable behaviours compatible with long-term health. What nutrition cannot do ; – Reverse the hormonal changes inherent to the menopausal transition. – Replace hormonal or pharmacological treatments when these are indicated. – Guarantee a systematic or uniform reduction of symptoms, particularly vasomotor symptoms. – Correct established pathologies on its own or compensate for a lack of adherence to lifestyle changes. – Justify restrictive, guilt-inducing, or excessively prescriptive approaches that are disconnected from the available level of evidence. Conclusion and clinical perspectives This review highlights that the menopausal transition repre - sents a pivotal period in women's health trajectories, during which nutrition may act as a modifiable lifestyle factor influencing car - diometabolic risk, bone and muscle health, and overall well-being. Available evidence indicates that these changes are neither uniform nor inevitable but rather unfold along heterogeneous trajectories shaped by individual, behavioral, and environmental determinants. In this context, nutrition should not be considered as an iso - lated intervention or as an exclusively symptom-oriented response, but rather as a modifiable lifestyle component that may contribute to reasoned prevention. Its impact appears to depend closely on the timing of intervention, baseline risk profile, and its integration within a comprehensive management strategy. The most consistent available evidence suggests that overall di - etary quality, particularly Mediterranean and DASH dietary pat - terns, contributes to the modulation of cardiometabolic risk and supports bone and muscle health, while effects on menopausal symptoms remain overall modest and heterogeneous. Nutritional interventions cannot replace other pillars of pre - vention, including regular physical activity, sleep and stress management, or hormonal and pharmacological treatments when indicated. Recognizing both the potential and the limits of nutrition is essential to avoid reductionist narratives and unreal - istic expectations. The menopausal transition may therefore be considered a window of opportunity to initiate or reinforce lifestyle strategies that are likely to influence long-term health trajectories. However, further well-designed longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to better identify the women most likely to benefit from targeted nutritional approaches and to clarify optimal imple - mentation strategies. Pending these data, an individualized, evidence-informed, and multidisciplinary approach remains the most clinically relevant framework to support women during the menopausal transition.
