Menopausal hormone therapy: Benefits and risks INTRODUCTION The mean age of menopause is 51 years, with 95 percent becoming menopausal between the ages of 45 to 55 years. During the menopause transition and postmenopause, most women experience vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes and night sweats). Systemic estrogen is the most effective treatment available for relief of hot flashes. Other symptoms associated with perimenopause and menopause that respond to systemic estrogen include sleep disturbances, mood lability/depression, and, in some cases, joint aches and pains. Postmenopausal women may also experience vulvovaginal symptoms including dyspareunia (genitourinary syndrome of menopause) which can be effectively treated by local administration of estrogen. An overview of the risks and benefits of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) will be provided here. The clinical features and diagnosis of menopause, as well as the management of women with hot flashes and other menopausal symptoms are reviewed in greater detail separately. The management of women with early menopause and primary ovarian insufficiency are also reviewed separately. ® AUTHORS: Kathryn A Martin, MD , Robert L Barbieri, MD SECTION EDITOR: Peter J Snyder, MD DEPUTY EDITOR: Jean E Mulder, MD All topics are updated as new evidence becomes available and our peer review process is complete. ESTIMATES OF OVERALL RISK BASED ON AGE Individualized risk assessment — We agree with most professional societies that advise not starting MHT without an individualized assessment of the risks and benefits [ 1-4 ]. When counseling symptomatic women considering MHT, the clinician should provide the best estimate of potential risks and benefits for up to five years of use based upon age-specific data [ 2,5,6 ]. (See "Treatment of menopausal symptoms with hormone therapy", section on 'Calculating risks' .) Vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes) are the most common indication for the use of MHT. As a result, almost all women who seek medical therapy for menopausal symptoms do so in their late 40s or 50s. MHT is no longer recommended for prevention of chronic disease such as coronary heart disease (CHD) or osteoporosis, preservation of cognitive function, or prevention of dementia. (See "Treatment of menopausal symptoms with hormone therapy", section on 'Clinical indications' .) Women ≥ 60 years — We agree with most professional societies that advise individualizing the use of MHT, particularly in women 60 to 69 years of age, since the benefit-risk ratio is less favorable in women ≥ 60 years than in women 50 to 59 years [ 1-3 ]. We avoid MHT in women ≥ 70 years [ 7 ]. (See 'Coronary heart disease' below and "Treatment of menopausal symptoms with hormone therapy", section on 'Calculating risks' and "Treatment of menopausal symptoms with hormone therapy", section on 'Extended use of MHT' .) The American Geriatric Society advises not starting MHT in women ≥ 65 years [ 4 ], since in the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) and subsequent meta-analyses, there was an excess risk of vascular events with conjugated equine estrogen (with or without medroxyprogesterone acetate ) compared with placebo [ 5,8-11 ]. The WHI reported adverse effects of menopausal MHT in older postmenopausal women ( ≥ 60 years or more than 10 years since menopause); this is not the age group that presents with new onset of menopausal symptoms. In addition, the data in the WHI are based on a hormone regimen that is no longer a preferred regimen. (See 'Cardiovascular effects' below and "Treatment of menopausal symptoms with hormone therapy", section on 'Our preferred regimens' .) Women 50 to 59 years — Most expert societies suggest MHT for younger postmenopausal women with moderate to severe symptoms and no contraindications to estrogen use [ 1,2,12 ]. CARDIOVASCULAR EFFECTS Coronary heart disease — MHT is not recommended for primary or secondary prevention of coronary heart disease [ 8,9,13-15 ]. Older menopausal women – In the Women's Health Initiative (WHI), the overall rate of coronary events was increased with combined CEE-medroxyprogesterone acetate therapy 2.3 versus 1.9 percent with placebo (hazard ratio [HR] 1.23, 95% CI 0.99-1.53). The estimated absolute excess risk was eight additional events per 10,000 person-years (95% CI 0.3 to 16) [ 16,17 ]. In a secondary analysis evaluating MHT in women with vasomotor symptoms by age group, women ≥ 70 years had increased risk of coronary events (HR 3.22, 95% CI 1.36- 7.63; 382 excess events per 10,000 person years) [ 7 ]. However, in women aged 60 to 69 years, CEE-medroxyprogesterone acetate did not increase risk (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.51-1.39; 40 fewer events per 10,000 person-years). ● In the unopposed estrogen trial, use of CEE did not appear to affect the incidence of CHD events over an average follow-up of 6.8 years (3.8 versus 4 percent with placebo, HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.78-1.16 [estimated absolute risk reduction of three fewer events per 10,000 person-years]) [ 9,16 ]. Additional reassuring data come from clinical trials in younger postmenopausal women: Stroke meta-analysis that included the WHI [ 20 ] all suggest that the timing of treatment initiation affects the risk of CHD; in the WHI, women who were <10 years since menopause or between the ages of 50 to 59 years did not have excess risk [ 7,16 ] or possibly had a reduction in risk [ 20 ]. The Kronos Early Estrogen Prevention Study (KEEPS), a four-year, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (in women ages 45 to 54 years), reported that, when combined with cyclical monthly oral progesterone , oral CEE (0.45 mg daily) or transdermal estrogen (50 mcg daily) reduced menopausal symptoms. Surrogate markers of atherosclerosis (coronary artery calcium and carotid intima-media thickness [CIMT]) were not significantly different in the hormone therapy (HT) and placebo groups [ 21 ]. ● In the Early versus Late Intervention Trial with Estradiol (ELITE), 643 postmenopausal women, stratified according to time since menopause (<6 or >10 years; early versus late, respectively), received oral estradiol (with progesterone for women with a uterus) or placebo for a median of five years [ 22 ]. Progression of subclinical atherosclerosis (measured as CIMT) was slower than placebo in the early intervention group, while rates of progression were similar to placebo in the late intervention group. Lipids — Oral estrogen has a proven beneficial effect on serum lipid concentrations [ 35-37 ], which can be negated in part by some progestogens such as medroxyprogesterone acetate . Micronized progesterone has a neutral effect. These effects appear to be independent of age, with similar results noted in younger and older postmenopausal women [ 37 ]. The effect of oral estradiol (1 mg/day) is similar to that of oral CEE 0.625 mg, while transdermal estradiol has a lesser effect than oral estrogen preparations [ 35 ]. The lack of effect with transdermal estrogen presumably reflects decreased exposure of the liver to estrogen (compared with oral therapy) due to avoidance of the first-pass effect on the liver. However, another study which treated women for much longer with varying doses of transdermal estradiol found that the serum total and LDL cholesterol concentrations decreased in a dose-dependent fashion after two years of therapy [ 38 ]. Blood pressure — Replacement doses of estrogen have little effect on blood pressure. The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) combined estrogen-progestin trial noted a small increase (1.5 mmHg) in systolic pressure compared with placebo [ 8 ]; a similar difference between the hormone and placebo groups of 1.1 mmHg was noted in the WHI trial of unopposed estrogen [ 9 ]. MORTALITY Available data suggest that MHT is not associated with an increase in mortality rates when compared with placebo. Younger menopausal women (ages 50 to 59) may actually have a reduction in all-cause mortality with MHT [ 39 ]. Early epidemiologic studies reported a reduction in mortality rates with MHT [ 8,9,16,39,40 ]. After excess cardiovascular risks were reported in early Women's Health Initiative (WHI) reports, there were concerns that MHT excess risk of VTE was observed in women taking transdermal estrogen (OR 1.2, 95% CI 0.1- 1.7), even in those with prothrombotic mutations or high body mass index (BMI) [ 31 ]. Similar results were reported in a population-based cohort study published after the meta- analysis [ 33 ]. Type of progestogen – In addition to the route of estrogen administration, risk may vary by type of progestogen (higher with medroxyprogesterone acetate , likely lower with micronized progesterone ) [ 34 ]. In a study of over one million postmenopausal women that included 2200 venous thromboembolic events, the relative risk of VTE in current hormone users versus nonusers was higher for women taking oral estrogen-progestogen than unopposed estrogen regimens (RR 2.07 versus 1.42) [ 34 ]. Transdermal estrogen users had no excess risk. Among the oral estrogen-progestogen users, risk was greater for regimens containing medroxyprogesterone acetate than other progestogens (RR 2.67 versus 1.91). ●

CANCER Breast cancer — In the WHI overall study population, combined CEE-medroxyprogesterone acetate use compared with placebo increased the risk of breast cancer (absolute excess risk of 9 cases per 10,000 person years [HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.01-1.53]) [ 5 ]. However, the absolute risk of breast cancer with combined MHT is very low with typical duration of use in postmenopausal women ages 50 to 59 years (eg, three additional cases per 1000 women with CEE plus medroxyprogesterone acetate for five years , therefore <1 per 1000/year) ( figure 1 ) [ 1,2,5 ]. In addition, hormone therapy use does not further increase after bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO) for BRCA 1 or 2 genetic variants [ 1 ]. A 2024 meta-analysis of randomized trials (over 14,000 participants) showed a decreased risk of breast cancer with use of estrogen alone (3.6 versus 4.7 percent for estrogen alone and placebo, respectively, RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.65-0.91) [ 43 ]. In observational studies with longer duration and a meta-analysis of observational studies, use of unopposed CEE was associated with a small but statistically significant increase in breast cancer risk (20- year risk of breast cancer [between the ages of 50 and 69 years] by approximately one in every 200 users of estrogen-only regimens) [ 44,45 ]. Current MHT regimens typically include lower doses of estrogen as well as a different type and route (eg, transdermal 17-beta estradiol [17-beta-E2]). In addition a synthetic progestin, medroxyprogesterone acetate , was used in the WHI trial, whereas micronized progesterone is now considered the progestogen of choice by most experts [ 2,12,46,47 ]. Limited observational data suggest that natural micronized progesterone may not be associated with additional risk of breast cancer [ 48 ]. It is thought that transdermal 17-beta-E2 and micronized progesterone may be associated with lower breast cancer risk than CEE and medroxyprogesterone acetate, although data are not yet conclusive. (See "Preparations for menopausal hormone therapy", section on 'Progestin preparations' .) We advise against MHT use in breast cancer survivors, given a potentially heightened risk of recurrence associated with MHT. (See "Overview of long-term complications of therapy in breast cancer survivors and patterns of relapse", section on 'Menopausal symptoms' .) Ovarian cancer — We do not consider ovarian cancer to be a major consideration when deciding to take MHT for symptomatic relief, because the absolute risk of ovarian cancer with MHT is very low. A nonsignificant increase in the risk of ovarian cancer was observed in the WHI with combined estrogen-progestin therapy (HR 1.6, 95% CI 0.8-3.2; 42 versus 27 cases per 100,000 person-years in the hormone and placebo groups, respectively) [ 49 ]. The excess risk was not statistically significant and would be approximately 0.75 cases per 1000 women treated for five years [ 6 ]. There were no differences in the distributions of tumor grade, stage, or histology. Because of the small number of ovarian cancer cases and the limited precision in estimating effects in the trial, these results should not affect a woman's decision to take MHT for symptomatic relief.

A meta-analysis of 52 epidemiologic studies including 21,488 postmenopausal women with ovarian cancer suggests that there is a small excess risk of ovarian cancer with MHT use [ 50 ]. Ovarian cancer risk was greater in ever-users versus never-users of MHT (relative risk [RR] 1.14, 95% CI 1.10-1.19). Risks were similar for estrogen-only and estrogen-progestin users. The calculated absolute excess risk associated with MHT was very low; five years of MHT use in women ages 50 to 54 years would result in approximately one additional ovarian cancer case per 1000 users and one ovarian cancer death per 1700 users. Age at initiation had little effect on RR, nor did smoking, body mass index, or past use of oral contraceptives. In one case-control study of 162 matched sets of women with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations, MHT did not appear to increase the risk of ovarian cancer; odds ratio (OR) with every use of HT was 0.93 (95% CI 0.56-1.56) [ 51 ]. Risk factors for ovarian cancer are discussed in detail elsewhere. (See "Epithelial carcinoma of the ovary, fallopian tube, and peritoneum: Incidence and risk factors" .) Endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma — In patients with a uterus, systemic estrogen therapy from any route (oral, transdermal patch, vaginal ring) without concurrent administration of a progestogen increases the risk of developing endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma. (See "Endometrial carcinoma: Epidemiology, risk factors, and prevention", section on 'Chronically increased estrogen levels or estrogenic activity' and "Endometrial hyperplasia or endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia: Clinical features, diagnosis, and differential diagnosis", section on 'Risk factors' .) Equivalent doses of transdermal and oral estrogen appear to have similar effects on the endometrium. However, in one study, ultra low-dose transdermal estrogen (14 mcg) and placebo showed similar low rates of endometrial proliferation and endometrial hyperplasia [ 52 ]. Recommendations for progestogen administration vary. (See "Treatment of menopausal symptoms with hormone therapy", section on 'Progestogens' .) Among women treated with estrogen, the excess risk of endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma can be largely abolished by concurrent therapy with a progestogen given in either a cyclic or continuous regimen [ 49,53-56 ]. In the estrogen-progestin WHI trial, more women in the MHT group required endometrial biopsies to assess vaginal bleeding (33 versus 6 percent for placebo), but there was no significant difference in endometrial cancer risk between the two groups (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.48-1.36) [ 49 ]. In the PEPI trial and in a review from the Cochrane Database, cyclic progestin (when given at least 12 days per month) was as effective as continuous low-dose progestin [ 55,57 ]. Shorter-duration progestin therapy (<10 days) may be less protective [ 57-62 ]. (See "Treatment of menopausal symptoms with hormone therapy", section on 'Progestogens' .)

Colorectal cancer — Initial data from WHI suggested a decrease in the risk of colorectal cancer with combined CEE-medroxyprogesterone acetate use but no effect with estrogen alone [ 63 ]. In a subsequent analysis with 23 years of follow-up, risks were similar in CEE + medroxyprogesterone acetate , CEE alone, and placebo groups [ 64 ]. This topic is discussed in greater detail separately. (See "Epidemiology and risk factors for colorectal cancer", section on 'Menopausal hormone therapy' .) Lung cancer — Women on combined estrogen-progestin therapy who developed non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) had a significantly shorter survival compared with those given placebo. (See "Females and lung cancer", section on 'Outcome' .) COGNITIVE FUNCTION AND DEMENTIA There is limited epidemiologic support for the hypothesis that estrogen preserves overall cognitive function in women. Meta-analyses of epidemiologic data [ 65 ] and randomized trials [ 66 ] have shown no benefit for prevention of dementia and no evidence of accelerated cognitive decline [ 67 ]. It is possible that there are domain-specific cognitive benefits (such as verbal memory) with MHT in younger postmenopausal women, but definitive evidence is lacking [ 68 ]. This topic is reviewed in more detail separately. (See "Estrogen and cognitive function" .) GALLBLADDER DISEASE A secondary analysis of data from the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) found a significantly increased risk of biliary tract disease among women using oral estrogen therapy [ 69 ]. Both trials showed a greater risk of any gallbladder disease or surgery in the groups randomized to oral estrogen (annual incidence rate 78 versus 47 per 10,000 person-years for estrogen alone and placebo, respectively, and 55 and 35 per 10,000 person-years in the estrogen plus progestin and placebo groups, respectively). In a subsequent analysis of women ages 50 to 59 (or <10 years menopausal), the attributable excess risk of cholecystitis was calculated to be 9.6 and 14.2 additional cases per 1000 women per five years of combined estrogen-progestin therapy or unopposed estrogen use, respectively [ 6 ]. Similar results were noted in the Heart and Estrogen/Progestin Replacement Study (HERS) trial, in which 2763 postmenopausal women with coronary heart disease (CHD) were randomly assigned to combined estrogen-progestin therapy or placebo [ 70 ]. After an average follow-up of four years, there was a marginally significant increase in biliary tract surgery in the treatment group (38 percent). The authors calculated that for every 185 women receiving hormone therapy (HT), one additional woman had biliary tract surgery per year. (See "Gallstones: Epidemiology, risk factors and prevention", section on 'Medications' .)

OTHER POSSIBLE BENEFITS Recurrent urinary tract infection — There is evidence that vaginal ( figure 3 ), but not systemic, estrogen therapy reduces the frequency of recurrent urinary tract infections in postmenopausal women [ 73-76 ]. (See "Genitourinary syndrome of menopause: Treatment of vulvovaginal atrophy", section on 'Vaginal estrogen therapy' and "Recurrent simple cystitis in female adults" .) Health-related quality of life — In postmenopausal women with vasomotor flushes, estrogen appears to improve quality of life [ 77-80 ]. Falls — Problems with balance may play a major role in the incidence of forearm fractures in postmenopausal women [ 84 ]. The incidence of Colles fractures increases markedly in women at age 50 years but remains stable in men up to the age of 80 years. A mechanism other than osteoporosis must be invoked to explain this observation because osteoporosis occurs gradually [ 85 ]. The fracture is usually caused by falling on an outstretched hand. Estrogen therapy may improve balance and reduce the tendency to fall, a change that could contribute to the associated decrease in fracture risk. Some studies have noted an improvement in balance with estrogen therapy [ 86 ], while others have not [ 87,88 ]. In one study of 19 postmenopausal women with vasomotor symptoms, for example, dynamic posturography revealed that the women had improved balance during treatment with estrogen [ 86 ]. (See "Menopausal hormone therapy in the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis" .) OTHER POSSIBLE RISKS Estrogen therapy may be associated with increases in the incidence of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and the Raynaud phenomenon. The data are insufficient for epilepsy. Although data are conflicting, there have been concerns that estrogen therapy is associated with an increased risk of meningiomas. Urinary incontinence – Estrogen may alleviate dyspareunia, recurrent cystitis, and vaginal/urethral atrophy and inflammation in postmenopausal women. Thus, oral estrogen with or without progestin should not be prescribed for this indication. In the Nurses' Health Study, for example, the relative risk (RR) of new-onset asthma in 36,094 postmenopausal women followed for 10 years was significantly greater in women taking estrogen as compared with those who were not (RR 1.5) [ 115 ]. This increased risk was dose related; it was statistically significant only at a dose greater than 0.625 mg/day of conjugated estrogens , probably because of the small numbers of women studied. ● Data are conflicting on whether estrogen therapy in postmenopausal asthmatic women causes a worsening of airway function. In one study of 15 postmenopausal women with mild to moderate asthma, the estrogen-treated women had subclinical worsening of disease activity (as measured by peak expiratory flow and spirometry) [ 116 ]. In contrast, in a second study of 20 postmenopausal asthmatic women, there were no differences in measures of airway obstruction after stopping and restarting estrogen therapy [ 117 ]. Thus, while estrogen is not contraindicated in women with obstructive lung disease, clinicians should be aware of the possibility of worsening bronchospasm. Furthermore, estrogen may be considered as an etiologic factor in women who develop asthma during therapy.

OTHER ISSUES Weight — Although women are often concerned that taking MHT will exacerbate the weight gain that occurs in midlife, a meta-analysis of 28 trials in 28,559 women found no evidence of an effect of unopposed estrogen or combined estrogen-progestin on body weight or body mass index [ 124 ]. GRAPHICS Figure 1: Risks and benefits of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) Updated summary of the effects of orally administered CEE alone (subjects post-hysterectomy) or combined with MPA (subjects with an intact uterus) in females ages 50 to 59 years during intervention phase of WHI. One set of analyses examined the risks and benefits of these agents in females ages 50 to 59 years. This figure plots these data, which are expressed here as excess risks and benefits per 1000 females using MHT for 5 years. Because females deciding to use MHT are more likely to continue this for a period of years rather than 1 year, this figure is constructed according to that assumption. WHI studies were not powered for age-related subset analyses, and none of the data presented in the figure are statistically significant. Nonetheless, this figure represents the best estimates that are available at the present time and are likely more reliable than similar estimates based on observational studies as reported previously in The Endocrine Society Scientific Statement. For endometrial cancer risk, no colored bars are shown because no increase or decrease in risk was seen in either treatment group compared with placebo. All subjects in the CEE-alone group had undergone hysterectomy and therefore had no risk. Combined CEE-MPA therapy in subjects with an intact uterus was not associated with an increased risk, suggesting that the MPA regimen provided adequate protection against estrogen-associated endometrial cancer.
