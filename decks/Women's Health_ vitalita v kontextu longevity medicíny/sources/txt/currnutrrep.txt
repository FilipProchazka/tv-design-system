Bischof 3,4 Clinical Definition of Menopause The Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop (STRAW + 10) describes the menstrual, reproductive, and hormonal changes that occur from the late reproductive stage through early postmenopause [ 1 ]. Collectively, these stages com - prise the menopausal transition, commonly referred to as perimenopause [ 1 ]. Perimenopause typically spans four to eight years and is defined not by stable estrogen defi - ciency but by hormonal variability, including intermittent estradiol surges and progressive progesterone withdrawal. Emerging evidence suggests that this phase represents a distinct metabolic transition during which risk for subse - quent dysglycemia, steatotic liver disease, and cardiovas - cular disease may increase, including in the absence of overt clinical symptoms [ 2 ]. The late reproductive stage (LRS) is associated with endocrinologic changes, including declining inhibin B and antimüllerian hormone, which contribute to reduced fecund - ability. In the years preceding the final menstrual period (FMP), perimenopause is characterized by fluctuations in estradiol, progesterone, and follicle stimulation hormone (FSH) that contribute to progressive changes in men - strual cycle pattern [ 3 – 5 ]. Dis - ruption of hypothalamic–ovarian axis feedback mechanisms can lead to cycle-to-cycle variability in lutenizing hormone (LH), FSH, estradiol, and progesterone concentrations [ 3 , 6 ]. Menopause is defined retrospectively, after 12 consecu - tive months without menses, reflecting the end of follicular ovarian function. Symptoms asso - ciated with the menopausal transition often begin before the FMP and persist for a median duration of 7.4 years [ 9 ]. Physical Symptoms of the Perimenopause The menopausal transition is associated with symptoms across multiple organ systems [ 1 , 9 ]. Central nervous sys - tem manifestations include vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes and night sweats), which are thought to arise in part from altered hypothalamic thermoregulatory control. Fluctuating estrogen levels may modify signaling in ther - moregulatory centers, narrowing the thermoneutral zone [ 9 ]. Estrogen also modulates neurotransmitters such as serotonin and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and is implicated in cerebral energy metabolism; variability in sex steroid concentrations has been associated with mood symp - toms, sleep disruption, cognitive complaints, and changes in sexual health [ 9 ].Declining estrogen contributes to changes in body composition associated with bothersome weight gain [ 4 , 9 ]. Estrogen withdrawal is associated with acceler - ated bone resorption and reduced bone density, loss of lean muscle mass, and progression of osteoarthritic symptoms [ 9 , 10 ]. Fluctuating estradiol levels may contribute to hypo - thalamic thermoregulatory instability, narrowing the ther - moneutral zone and promoting vasomotor symptoms that fragment sleep [ 11 , 12 ]. Declining estrogen and rising FSH levels have been associated with impaired sleep quality and reduced sleep efficiency [ 13 , 14 ]. Decreased collagen production and alterations in estro - gen/androgen ratios may contribute to hair and skin changes. Finally, urogenital tissues are highly estrogen sensitive, and reduced estrogen is associated with progressive vaginal dry - ness, dyspareunia, urinary symptoms, and increased suscep - tibility to urinary tract infections [ 9 ]. Sleep disorders such as insomina are common during perimenopause and are associated with reduced quality of life, fatigue and impaired work functioning [ 11 , 13 ]. The longitudinal Study of Wom - en’s Health Across the Nation (SWAN) estimated the risk of sleep problems in perimenopausal women to be higher by a factor of 1.5 compared with premenopausal women [ 15 ]. Sleep disruption continues to persist into menopause reported by 16% to 47% of perimenopausal women, with prevalence increasing to 35% to 60% after menopause [ 11 , 16 ]. In observational studies, sleep disruption is also associ - ated with worsening mood symptoms, impaired attention, and subjective memory concerns [ 13 ]. Chronic sleep dis - ruption has been linked to weight gain, obesity, metabolic dysfunction, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease in broader populations [ 17 , 18 ]. Physiologic Basis of Perimenopausal Symptoms and Metabolic Risk Hormonal Nexus: Estrogen, Inflammation, and Adipose Dysfunction A central feature of perimenopause is hormonal volatility, which has been associated with metabolic changes that may not be fully captured by experimental models of fixed estro - gen deprivation. During this period, estrogen receptor-α (ERα) signaling in skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tis - sue may become less responsive, contributing to a state of relative hormone resistance that can precede sustained estrogen deficiency. In parallel, declining progesterone may reduce anti-inflammatory signaling, potentially favoring expansion of visceral adipose tissue even in the absence of large changes in total body weight. Hypertrophied visceral adipocytes increase delivery of free fatty acids to the por - tal circulation and may promote macrophage polarization toward a more proinflammatory phenotype, contributing to elevations in interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α [ 19 ]. This low-grade inflammatory milieu may, in turn, reinforce visceral adiposity and impair insulin signaling through mul - tiple pathways. Deterioration of Systemic Metabolism: Energy Expenditure and Metabolic Inflexibility Longitudinal data from several prospective cohorts have consistently demonstrated that the perimenopausal transi - tion is accompanied by a steepening decline in insulin-stim - ulated glucose disposal, an effect that remains significant after adjustment for chronological age and body mass index [ 20 , 21 ]. Proposed mechanisms include reduced glycogen synthesis in skeletal muscle, impaired suppression of hepatic glucose production, and progressive deterioration of pancre - atic beta-cell function [ 22 ]. Fasting glucose often remains within the normal reference range until late perimenopause, whereas postprandial glucose excursions and fasting insu - lin levels may increase earlier. Beyond glucose dysregulation, the perimenopausal transition is associated with a shift toward a more athero - genic lipid profile. Total cholesterol, low-density lipopro - tein cholesterol, and triglycerides tend to increase, while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol declines, patterns that appear to persist after adjustment for age and adiposity [ 4 , 25 ]. Proposed mechanisms include estrogen-dependent changes in hepatic lipase and lipoprotein lipase activity, as well as increased flux of free fatty acids from visceral adipose tissue. Untargeted metabolomic studies have also reported a signature of elevated branched-chain amino acids (leucine, isoleucine, valine) and aromatic amino acids (phe - nylalanine, tyrosine) emerging during perimenopause and persisting into postmenopause [ 26 , 27 ]. Elevated branched- chain amino acids have been associated with future insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes independent of traditional risk factors [ 28 ]. This amino acid profile correlates with de novo lipogenesis in the liver and may represent an early bio - marker of metabolic deterioration during the transition. Resting metabolic rate declines with age. Whether the hormonal fluctuations of perimenopause independently affect energy metabolism—beyond the effects of chrono - logical aging, changes in body composition, and lifestyle factors—remains debated. Several longitudinal studies sug - gest these changes are not fully explained by chronological aging or body composition alone; rather they reflect unique endocrine transitions with direct metabolic consequences. Mechanistic research points to disrupted estrogen receptor signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroendocrine uncoupling as contributors to hypometabolic states during perimenopause [ 29 , 30 ]. However, other studies suggest that menopausal hormonal changes may not exert a signifi - cant independent effect on resting energy expenditure after adjustment for age and body composition. For example, Karppinen et al. reported that adjusted resting energy expen - diture declined by approximately 3.9 kcal/day per year of age, without clear evidence of an additional independent effect attributable to menopausal status [ 31 ]. In addtion to the hormonal and metabolic changes, body composition also shifts. Redistribution of adiposity toward the visceral compartment has been reported, with visceral adipose tissue increasing by approximately 8.2% per year in the two years preceding the final menstrual period and by 5.8% per year thereafter [ 32 ]. Cross-sectional pooled analyses similarly suggest that postmenopausal women have approximately 26.9 cm² greater visceral fat area and 4.63 cm greater waist circumference than premenopausal women [ 33 ]. In SWAN, lean mass changed from + 0.2% per year before to − 0.2% per year during the transition, representing an average loss of approximately 0.2 kg [ 34 ]. Bone min - eral density also declines across the transition, with femoral neck bone loss accelerating from − 0.51% per year at ages 45–49 to − 1.39% per year at ages 50–54, and lumbar spine bone loss accelerating from − 0.56% to − 1.33% per year over the same interval [ 35 ]. Collectively, these data sug - gest that perimenopause is characterized less by weight gain than by central fat accumulation, modest loss of lean tissue, age-related metabolic slowing, and accelerated skeletal loss. The cumulative effect of these systemic alterations is associated with higher prevalence of metabolic syn - drome across the menopausal transition. A large system - atic review and meta-analysis of observational studies reported a pooled prevalence of metabolic syndrome in postmenopausal women of 37.2% (95% confidence inter - val 35.0-39.3%), corresponding to a 1.5- to 4-fold higher risk compared with premenopausal women [ 36 ]. In obser - vational datasets, changes occur across all five components of the syndrome (waist circumference, triglycerides, blood pressure, fasting glucose, and high-density lipoprotein cho - lesterol). Mechanistically, estrogen withdrawal has been linked to activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and increased vascular smooth muscle tone, which may contribute to higher blood pressure independent of obesity [ 37 ]. Consistent with this physiology, markers of cardiovascular disease risk—including coronary artery calcification, carotid intima-media thickness, and incident heart failure with preserved ejection fraction—have been reported to increase during the perimenopausal years in some cohorts [ 4 , 8 , 25 , 38 – 41 ]. Cerebral Metabolism and Neuroendocrine Integration The metabolic consequences of perimenopause may extend to the central nervous system. As previously described, estrogen and progesterone impact multiple neurohormonal signaling pathways. Some frameworks distinguish chrono - logical from endocrinological influences on brain aging, with perimenopause proposed as a period during which these processes intersect. Positron emission tomography studies have reported reductions in cerebral glucose utili - zation during perimenopause, particularly in the hypothala - mus, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex—regions that are rich in estrogen receptors and participate in metabolic and

Reduced brain glucose metabo - lism has been described alongside microglial activation and elevated central proinflammatory cytokines, a profile that could plausibly contribute to fatigue, sleep disruption, and cognitive symptoms reported during the transition [ 43 – 45 ]. The hypothalamus integrates peripheral metabolic signals (e.g., leptin, insulin, ghrelin) with neuroendocrine outputs, including gonadotropin-releasing hormone and corticotro - pin-releasing hormone [ 46 , 47 ]. Disruption of hypothalamic signaling during perimenopause has been hypothesized to influence appetite regulation, visceral adiposity, and corti - sol dynamics, which may contribute to impaired peripheral insulin sensitivity. Nutritional Strategies to Address Perimenopausal Metabolic Changes The hormonal and metabolic shifts of perimenopause are associated with changes relevant to skeletal health, muscle mass, gastrointestinal physiology, and cardiometabolic risk. Nutritional strategies have therefore been exam - ined as potential adjuncts to other interventions across the menopausal transition. The following section summarizes evidence and guideline-based considerations for selected dietary components and supplements that have been stud - ied in relation to perimenopausal and early postmenopausal outcomes. Dietary Strategies Recognition that perimenopause is an active, hormonally mediated metabolic state has implications for prevention and treatment strategies. Lifestyle interventions are com - monly recommended as initial management; however, intervention components and expected response may dif - fer across the menopausal transition [ 48 ]. The Mediterra - nean dietary pattern is among the most consistently studied nutritional approaches during menopause transition. An European Menopause and Andropause Society (EMAS) position statement synthesizing observational and inter - ventional evidence concluded that adherence to a Mediter - ranean diet may improve vasomotor symptoms, mood, and depressive symptoms in midlife women, potentially through anti-inflammatory effects, improved insulin sensitivity, and favorable neurovascular modulation [ 49 ]. Some studies suggest that caloric restriction alone may be insufficient to improve metabolic flexibility in all indi - viduals, whereas combined approaches incorporating mod - est energy deficit (approximately 300–500 kcal/day), dietary patterns higher in unsaturated fats and fiber, and adequate protein intake are associated with improvements in visceral adiposity and insulin sensitivity [ 50 ]. Intermittent fasting in menopause has been evaluated in a small number of clinical studies, mostly using time- restricted eating. A recent quasi-randomized controlled trial showed that combining time restricted eating (TRE) with exercise improved menopausal symptoms and quality of life compared to exercise alone, although vasomotor symptoms were unchanged [ 51 ]. Earlier controlled studies in meno - pausal women demonstrated that TRE plus exercise leads to greater reductions in body mass index (BMI) and improve - ments in body composition than exercise alone, while ran - domized and subgroup analyses indicate that weight loss and metabolic benefits of TRE are similar in pre- and post - menopausal women [ 52 ]. Protein Intake Loss of lean mass during and after the menopausal tran - sition has motivated interest in protein adequacy as a potentially modifiable factor. The European Society for Clinical and Economic Aspects of Osteoporosis and Osteoarthritis (ESCEO) and the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) recom - mend 1.0–1.2 g/kg body weight/day for healthy older adults, with intake distributed across meals (20–25 g of high-quality protein per meal) to support muscle protein synthesis [ 53 , 54 ]. Leucine-rich protein sources (e.g., dairy, eggs, legumes, and fish) have been emphasized in expert guidance, and resistance exercise is consistently associated with augmentation of the anabolic response to dietary protein [ 54 , 55 ]. While adequate protein is necessary, it is not sufficient to maintain or increase muscle mass and must be combined with adequate physi - cal activity to increase muscle mass [ 56 , 57 ]. Exercise interventions that combine resistance training (to pre - serve fat-free mass) with aerobic modalities, including higher-intensity interval training, have been associated with improvements in metabolic flexibility and postpran - dial lipid handling [ 58 ]. Overall, earlier initiation of life - style interventions during the perimenopausal years may be associated with larger effect sizes than interventions begun after metabolic syndrome is established, although direct comparative trials remain limited. These gastrointes - tinal changes have been linked mechanistically to the estrobolome, the ensemble of gut bacterial genes encoding β-glucuronidase enzymes that deconjugate estrogens and facilitate enterohepatic reabsorption [ 25 , 59 ]. Perturbations in this axis during perimenopause could plausibly influence circulating estrogen bioavailability and downstream meta - bolic risk, although causal pathways in humans remain an active area of investigation. At a population level, higher dietary fiber intake has been associated with lower metabolic syndrome risk and mor - tality in postmenopausal cohorts. In National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) analyses, each 1 g/1,000 kcal increase in fiber density was associated with a 4% reduction in the odds of metabolic syndrome, with partial mediation through inflammatory markers [ 61 ]. In addition, fiber intake above 7.9 g/day was associated with lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [ 62 ]. Mecha - nistically, fiber fermentation supports microbial diversity relevant to estrobolome function, and short-chain fatty acids may reinforce intestinal barrier integrity, potentially coun - tering menopause-associated gut barrier disruption [ 59 ]. Consistent with broader dietary guidance, recommended intakes are commonly cited in the range of 21–25 g/day from whole-food sources such as legumes, whole grains, vegetables, and fruit [ 63 , 64 ]. Alcohol Moderate alcohol consumption (10–14 g/day for women and 20–28 g/day for men or roughly 1 drink/day in women, 1–2 in men) has historically been considered a defining feature of the traditional Mediterranean diet—particularly when consumed with meals—but its inclusion is now under significant scientific scrutiny due to emerging evidence about associated health risks [ 65 – 67 ]. Alcohol consumption—especially at higher levels—is linked to poorer menopause-specific quality of life scores, particularly affecting vasomotor (hot flashes), psychosocial, and physical domains. Heavy drinking increases the risk of bothersome vasomotor symptoms, while light-to-moderate intake shows mixed or neutral associations [ 68 , 69 ]. Across many large studies, even light to moderate alcohol intake is associated with a gradual increase in breast cancer risk in women [ 70 ]. The proposed mechanism by which alcohol heightened the risk for breast cancer in women it believed to be related to the alcohol increasing estradiol and testosterone and lowering sex hormone binding globulin, both in perimenopause and after menopause; these small hormonal shifts are thought to contribute to breast cancer risk [ 71 ]. Micronutrients Calcium and Vitamin D Accelerated bone loss during the menopausal transition underscores the importance of adequate calcium and vita - min D intake. Many guidelines recommend total calcium intake of approximately 1,000–1,200 mg/day (preferably from dietary sources) and vitamin D intake of 600–1,000 IU/day for postmenopausal women [ 72 , 73 ]. Cholecalcif - erol (vitamin D3) is generally favored over ergocalciferol (D2) for raising and maintaining serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentrations [ 74 ]. Calcium plus vitamin D supplemen - tation has been reported to confer modest benefits for bone mineral density in some populations, particularly among older women and those with high adherence; however, rou - tine supplementation for all postmenopausal women is not uniformly recommended [ 72 , 75 ]. When dietary calcium intake is insufficient, supplemental calcium may be used to meet total intake targets, with attention to limiting higher supplemental doses given associations with nephrolithiasis risk in some studies [ 75 ]. Vitamin K Vitamin K2 (menaquinones) is involved in γ-carboxylation of osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein, processes that influ - ence bone mineralization and may modulate vascular cal - cification pathways [ 76 , 77 ]. A 2022 meta-analysis of 16 randomized controlled trials reported small improvements in lumbar spine bone mineral density with vitamin K2 sup - plementation (mean difference 1.02%, p = 0.0006), whereas evidence for fracture reduction was inconsistent across studies [ 76 , 78 ]. In contrast, a 3-year randomized trial in osteopenic postmenopausal women found that menaqui - none-7 (375 μg/day) added to calcium and vitamin D did not prevent bone mineral density loss [ 79 ]. Notably, many of the trials reporting benefit were conducted in Japan, which may limit generalizability across populations and dietary contexts [ 78 ]. Magnesium Magnesium has established roles in bone metabolism and vitamin D activation with 60% of it being stored in bone and notable impact on osteoclast and osteoblast activity, and notabely many women have been found to consume less than the RDA [ 80 – 82 ]. While higher magnesium intake has been associated with higher bone mineral density (BMD), initial review of cohort studies did not find that higher magnesium intake was associated with reduced fracture risk [ 82 ]. Taken together, available data support consideration of dietary patterns that address multiple physiologic domains relevant to the menopausal transition. While calcium and vita - min D targets are most directly linked to skeletal outcomes, additional dietary factors—including adequate high-quality protein intake and higher fiber consumption from diverse plant sources—may plausibly influence concurrent trajecto - ries in muscle mass, gut-mediated estrogen metabolism, and cardiometabolic risk [ 25 , 59 ]. Evidence for vitamin K2 and magnesium remains mixed and appears context dependent, underscoring the need for standardized trials in perimeno - pausal populations with clinically meaningful outcomes. Additional Supplements Interest in herbal and non-pharmacologic supplements for perimenopausal symptom management is common, but the evidence base remains heterogeneous and, for many agents, insufficient to support routine use. Creatine has been exam - ined primarily in younger athletic populations and, in midlife women, more often in postmenopausal cohorts; long-term data specific to perimenopause remain limited [ 84 ]. Soy isoflavones act as selective estrogen receptor modu - lators and have been evaluated for menopausal symptoms, with effects that appear modest on average. Thes phytoestro - gens are commonly used for vasomotor symptoms, however their benefit were not conclusively confirmed in a Cochrane analyses, although they found that genistein-rich soy extracts may reduce hot flashes in some women [ 85 ]. Across trials, heterogeneity is substantial, placebo responses are common, and benefits may attenuate in longer-duration studies [ 86 ]. Observational data from the InterLACE consortium suggest an association between frequent soy food consumption and lower likelihood of subsequent vasomotor symptoms [ 87 ]. Collectively, current evidence supports a potential symp - tomatic benefit in some individuals, although effect sizes are generally small and appear time dependent. Accordingly, for most supplements, conclusions are best framed as provisional pending adequately powered, standardized randomized trials with clinically meaningful end points. Other Therapeutic Implications Menopausal hormone therapy has a complex history in metabolic medicine, but evidence for initiation during the perimenopausal window has become more favorable in some studies and may have metabolic implications. When initiated during this period, estrogen therapy (with or with - out a progestogen, as indicated) has been associated with reductions in visceral adipose accumulation, improvements in insulin sensitivity, lower fasting glucose, and attenuation of increases in blood pressure. Reported benefits appear larger among women who begin therapy before established insulin resistance or overt diabetes. However, hormone therapy is not a universal metabolic intervention: it does not reverse advanced hepatic fibrosis, and effects on body weight are generally modest, with some studies suggesting prevention of further gain rather than induction of loss [ 88 , 89 ]. Clinical decision-making therefore typically weighs potential metabolic benefits against individual risks, includ - ing breast cancer, venous thromboembolism, and stroke. A post hoc analysis of the Women’s Health Initiative suggested that baseline metabolic syndrome status may modify coro - nary outcomes, with coronary event risk significantly higher among women with metabolic syndrome who received hor - mone therapy, compared to women without metabolic syn - drome [ 90 ]. These findings underscore the importance of risk stratification and individualized assessment. Future Directions and Unresolved Questions Understanding of perimenopause-associated metabolic change has advanced substantially; however, several ques - tions remain unresolved. First, the optimal timing, duration, and formulation of hormone therapy for metabolic outcomes (as distinct from symptom relief) require dedicated random - ized controlled trials with prespecified metabolic end points, rather than reliance on secondary analyses of cardiovascular outcome studies. Second, the potential role of genotype- informed precision nutrition during perimenopause remains largely unexplored. Polymorphisms in genes involved in estrogen metabolism (COMT, rs4680), one-carbon metabo - lism (MTHFR, rs1801133 and rs1801131), and fucosylation (FUT2, rs602662 and rs601338) have been associated with interindividual differences in weight gain, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance, suggesting possible targets for personal - ized prevention strategies [ 91 – 94 ]. Third, the gut microbi - ome undergoes compositional shifts during perimenopause, including reduced diversity and an increase in proinflam - matory taxa; whether microbiome-targeted interventions (e.g., prebiotics, probiotics, or fecal microbiota transplanta - tion) can meaningfully alter metabolic trajectories remains uncertain. Fourth, associations between perimenopausal metabolic changes and later neurodegenerative outcomes, particularly in apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4) carriers, warrant prospective investigation. Conclusion Perimenopause is a hormonally volatile state associated with a cascade of metabolic changes, including increasing visceral adiposity, declining insulin sensitivity, atherogenic dyslipidemia, hepatic steatosis, reductions in energy expen - diture, and alterations in central neuroendocrine regulation. Many of these changes appear to precede clinical diagno - sis by several years, highlighting perimenopause as both a window of vulnerability and a potential opportunity for earlier risk identification. Further work is needed to define optimal approaches to stage-specific screening and to deter - mine which interventions most effectively attenuate adverse cardiometabolic trajectories when initiated during the peri - menopausal years.
